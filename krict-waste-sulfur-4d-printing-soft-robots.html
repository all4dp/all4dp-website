Korean team turns waste sulfur into a recyclable 4D printing material

Sulfur-rich plastics made from refinery by-product respond to heat, light and magnets, and can be melted and printed again.

16 March 2026 · 2 min read · all4dp editorial

Illustration: all4dp

Researchers at the Korea Research Institute of Chemical Technology (KRICT), with Hanyang University and Sejong University, have 4D printed structures from sulfur-rich polymers. The sulfur comes from the by-product of petroleum refining, of which the world produces tens of millions of tonnes a year.

What is new

Sulfur plastics are normally too tightly cross-linked to extrude. The team loosened the polymer network so that it flows through a nozzle and can be printed into complex shapes, while keeping the chemistry that makes the material responsive.

What it can do

  • Shape memory: printed parts change shape when heated or lit, with no added mechanical parts.
  • Light welding: an 8-second near-infrared laser pulse joins printed parts without adhesive.
  • Magnetic motion: with 20% magnetic particles, soft robots under 1 cm long move by following a magnetic field.
  • Recycling: used parts can be melted and fed back in as printing material.

Why it matters

Most 4D printing materials are speciality polymers made for the purpose. This work starts from an industrial waste stream and ends with a material that responds to three different stimuli and can be reused. That combination addresses two common criticisms of smart materials at once: cost and end-of-life.

The lead researcher, Dr. Dong-Gyun Kim, describes the study as the first example of upcycling industrial sulfur waste into advanced robotic materials. That claim is the team's own.
