Heat, water, light, magnets: the triggers of 4D printing

Each stimulus has strengths and limits. Choosing one is the first design decision.

Updated 13 November 2026 · 2 min read · all4dp editorial

Illustration: all4dp

A 4D-printed part does nothing until something triggers it. The choice of trigger decides where the part can be used, how fast it moves and how precisely it can be controlled.

Heat

The most common trigger, used with shape memory polymers, shape memory alloys and liquid crystal elastomers. It is simple and reliable: hot water, warm air or an oven will do. The drawbacks are that the whole part usually has to be heated, large parts respond slowly, and some settings, such as the inside of the body, tolerate only a narrow temperature range.

Water and humidity

Hydrogels and some composites swell as they absorb water. Printing two materials that swell by different amounts side by side makes a strip bend, which is the basis of many self-folding designs. The response is reversible but slow, and it depends on the surrounding conditions.

Light

Light can be aimed at one spot and switched in an instant, so it gives local and remote control. It either heats the material where it lands or drives a chemical change directly. It cannot reach deep into opaque parts. One recent study used a short near-infrared pulse to weld printed pieces together.

Magnetic fields

Parts filled with magnetic particles respond within fractions of a second, through walls and tissue, with no contact. This makes magnetic actuation attractive for small robots and medical tools. It needs an external magnet or coil, and force falls quickly with distance.

Electric current

Conductive fillers or embedded wires let a part be heated by passing current through it. This allows precise electronic control and suits devices that already carry power, at the cost of wiring and a power source.

pH and chemicals

Some hydrogels swell or shrink as acidity changes or in particular solvents. These triggers matter mainly in biomedical and sensing uses, where the surroundings themselves provide the signal.

More than one

Many recent materials respond to several triggers, so one can be used to set a shape and another to release it, or different parts of a device can be driven independently. Combining triggers is one of the most active directions in the field.
