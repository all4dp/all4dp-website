Materials used in 4D printing: the four main families

What each one responds to, how it is printed, and where it fits.

Updated 13 November 2026 · 2 min read · all4dp editorial

Illustration: all4dp

Almost every 4D-printed part is made from one of four material families. They differ in what triggers them, how much they move and how strong they are.

FamilyUsual triggerReversible?Typical printing process
Shape memory polymersHeatUsually one-wayFilament extrusion, resin printing
HydrogelsWater, temperature, pHYesDirect ink writing, resin printing
Liquid crystal elastomersHeat, lightYesDirect ink writing
Shape memory alloysHeatOne-way or two-wayLaser powder bed fusion

Shape memory polymers

The workhorse of the field. These plastics are stiff when cool and rubbery above a transition temperature. They can be deformed while warm, cooled to lock the shape, and reheated to recover. PLA and polyurethane-based grades print on ordinary desktop machines, which is why so much research uses them. Read more in shape memory polymers explained.

Hydrogels

Water-swollen polymer networks. They move by taking up or releasing water, so they can produce large, reversible changes, but they are soft and relatively slow. They suit biomedical uses and soft devices. Recent examples include a hydrogel smart skin and a gel that also cleans water.

Liquid crystal elastomers

Rubbery networks containing rod-like molecules that line up during printing. On heating or illumination the alignment is lost and the material contracts along the print path, then relaxes back. They behave like artificial muscle: fast, reversible and able to do work.

Shape memory alloys

Metals, most often nickel-titanium, that recover large strains through a change in crystal structure. They are far stronger than polymers and are printed from powder with a laser. Controlling composition during printing is difficult, since small changes shift the transition temperature.

Composites and fillers

Adding particles or fibres widens the choice of trigger. Magnetic particles allow remote, fast actuation. Conductive fillers allow heating by electric current. Continuous fibres add stiffness and can themselves drive shape change, as in composite wind turbine blades. New feedstocks are also appearing, including polymers made from waste sulfur.
