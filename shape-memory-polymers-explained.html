Shape memory polymers explained

How a plastic remembers its shape, and the two numbers used to score it.

Updated 13 November 2026 · 2 min read · all4dp editorial

Illustration: all4dp

Shape memory polymers (SMPs) are the most widely used materials in 4D printing. They can be fixed in a temporary shape and return to their original shape when heated.

How the memory works

An SMP contains two kinds of structure. Permanent links between polymer chains define the original shape. A second, switchable phase is stiff when cold and mobile when hot. Below the transition temperature the switchable phase freezes the chains in place. Above it they are free to move, and the permanent links pull the part back to where it started.

For many SMPs the transition is the glass transition temperature, written Tg. For others it is a melting temperature.

The programming cycle

  1. Heat the printed part above its transition temperature.
  2. Deform it into the temporary shape.
  3. Cool it while holding the deformation.
  4. Release. The part keeps the temporary shape.
  5. Reheat. The part recovers its printed shape.

Deforming above the transition is called hot programming. Deforming below it, called cold programming, is also possible and gives different recovery behaviour. In filament printing, the printing process itself stretches the polymer and stores strain, so a part can change shape on first heating without a separate programming step.

Two numbers to know

Shape fixity ratio measures how well the temporary shape is held after the load is removed: the strain that remains, divided by the strain that was applied.

Shape recovery ratio measures how completely the original shape returns on reheating: the strain recovered, divided by the strain that was applied.

Both are given as percentages, and values above 90% are considered good. When a paper reports, for example, 94% fixity and 85% recovery, these are the quantities meant.

What affects performance

  • Programming temperature and how far the part is deformed
  • Print settings such as infill density, layer height and print direction
  • Recovery temperature and heating rate
  • The number of cycles, since performance usually declines with repetition

Limits

Most SMPs are one-way: they need reprogramming after each recovery. They produce modest recovery forces compared with metals, and heat has to reach the whole part, which slows large pieces. Adjusting the chemistry can move the transition temperature, which is how one group designed stents that deploy at 37 °C.
