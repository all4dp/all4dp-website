What is 4D printing? A plain guide

3D printing makes a shape. 4D printing makes a shape that changes later, on cue.

Updated 13 November 2026 · 2 min read · all4dp editorial

Illustration: all4dp

4D printing means 3D printing an object from a material that responds to its environment, so the object changes shape, stiffness or function after it is printed. The fourth dimension is time.

The short version

A normal 3D-printed part stays as printed. A 4D-printed part is designed to do something afterwards: a flat strip curls into a hook, a tube opens wider, a lattice softens. Nothing mechanical drives the change. It comes from the material and from how the part was designed and printed.

Three ingredients

  1. A responsive material. Shape memory polymers, hydrogels, liquid crystal elastomers and shape memory alloys are the main families. See our materials guide.
  2. A design that turns the response into motion. Hinges, bilayers that bend because one side swells more than the other, and lattices that fold are common patterns.
  3. A trigger. Heat is the most common. Water, light, magnetic fields and electric current are also used. See stimuli explained.

Where the name comes from

The term was introduced in 2013 by Skylar Tibbits of MIT, who showed printed strands that folded themselves into shapes under water. The field has since grown from demonstrations to engineered parts, such as stents that open at body temperature.

One-way and two-way change

Many 4D-printed parts change once: they are set into a temporary shape and return to the printed one when triggered. This is one-way shape memory. Some materials, such as liquid crystal elastomers and many hydrogels, switch back and forth every time the trigger is applied and removed. That reversible behaviour is what actuators and soft robots need.

What it is used for

  • Medical devices that are inserted small and open in place
  • Soft robots and grippers with no motors
  • Deployable structures for space and packaging
  • Mechanical metamaterials that absorb impact or change stiffness
  • Laboratory models of tissue, such as self-folding ducts for cancer research

What holds it back

Responsive materials are often weaker than ordinary engineering plastics, shape change can be slow, and performance may fade over many cycles. Predicting the final shape also takes careful modelling. Most applications are still in research, with medical devices and aerospace closest to practical use.
